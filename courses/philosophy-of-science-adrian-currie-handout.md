---

### ★ TAKE HOME

> **If we want creative science, we want funny science.**

1. Imagination is hard and science is conservative — seek tools for creative science.
2. Most “science & humour” work is pedagogy/communication; the gap is **knowledge generation**.
3. *Incongruity* → humour can be *revelatory* and motivate inquiry.
4. Humour also creates productive distance and motivates serious play.
5. Humour can be part of a thought experiment’s *form* and help *grasp*.
6. Mirth is an epistemic emotion: signals getting the joke and motivates exploration.

### 01 // FRAME — aesthetics → PhilSci

**CLAIM 0.** Currie situates the talk in a program (with Alice Murphy, Derek Turner, and others) that brings **aesthetics** more centrally into philosophy of science; today’s aesthetic object is **humor / mirth**, not beauty-as-such.

**ARGUMENT 0.**  
(P1) Creative science needs cognitive tools beyond “more data / stricter method.”  
(P2) Humor looks like a candidate tool once we treat it as epistemically structured (incongruity, play-distance, mirth).  
(C) So PhilSci should analyze humor as part of scientific *practice and products*, not only as PR.

**EXAMPLE 0 (opening hook — cats in 2D boxes).**  
Late-2010s internet craze: cats sit in tape-drawn “boxes.” Funny *and* question-raising (“how do cats see the world?”).  
**What it is for:** seed the claim that humor can *launch inquiry*, not merely entertain.

**UPSHOT 0.** The talk is **normative + constructive**: not a sociology of jokes, but an argument that science **should** make room for funny practice if it wants creativity.

---

### 02 // PRESSURE — creativity under pressure

### I.A Dual challenge to imagination

**CLAIM 1.** Imagination is a poor “default” epistemic engine for science because (i) the space of imaginings is vast, yet (ii) humans mostly imagine near their experiential / cultural repertoire—so imagination is **narrow and arbitrary**.

**ARGUMENT 1.**  
(P1) Unconceived alternatives matter for science; we need epistemically *relevant* novel options (pursuit-worthy, not mere fantasy).  
(P2) Human imagination systematically undersamples that space (experience-bound; culturally local).  
(C) Therefore creativity needs **structured tools** that push beyond habitual imaginings.

**EXAMPLE 1 (night-sky metaphor).**  
Possible imaginings ≈ infinite night sky; likely imaginings ≈ the few stars we actually look at.  
**What it is for:** make “arbitrariness of imagination” vivid without denying imagination’s occasional brilliance.

---

### I.B Science’s productive conservatism

**CLAIM 2.** Modern science is **conservatively structured**—and that conservatism is often *good* for focus and reliability—but it also chokes risky creativity.

**ARGUMENT 2.**  
(P1) Peer review / grants require acceptance by similarly trained peers → disciplinary sameness.  
(P2) Science is hierarchical; early-career researchers have little room for weird bets; “publish or perish” punishes risk.  
(P3) We should not abolish careful conservatism wholesale.  
(C) We need tools that **mitigate** conservatism’s downside without destroying its upside.

**EXAMPLE 2 (institutional pressures).**  
Same-training peers as legitimacy gatekeepers; cutthroat natural-science publishing incentives.  
**What it is for:** locate the creativity problem as **structural**, not as “scientists aren’t clever enough.”

**UPSHOT I.** Currie’s opening normative wedge: **humor could be (sometimes is) a tool for mitigating these challenges.**

---

### 03 // GAP — what existing humour work leaves out

**CLAIM 3.** Extant treatments of humor in science mostly cover **pedagogy**, **public communication**, and **STS boundary / community work**—not **knowledge generation / creativity**.

**ARGUMENT 3.**  
(P1) Ig Nobel–style framing and “funny titles” literature mainly sell *public interest / accessibility / message packaging*.  
(P2) STS cases (e.g. Wylie) explain jokes as *social* work (boundary marking, community bonding, power over evidence).  
(P3) The opening cat case looks like humor helping *generate a research idea / design*.  
(C) Therefore a PhilSci gap remains: humor’s role in **creative epistemic work**.

**EXAMPLE 3A (Ig Nobel / funny titles).**  
Ig Nobel purpose: promote public interest, make research accessible, highlight odd relevance—not theorize discovery. Title genre Currie likes: hyper-specific (“One week habitation of two humans… with two goats and twenty-three crops”).  
**What it is for:** show *outward-facing* humor without creativity theory.

**EXAMPLE 3B (Caitlin Wylie, *Preparing Dinosaurs*).**  
Fossil preparators’ recurring joke: a prep mistake “creates a new species” (paleontologists may read tool marks as morphology). Wylie: the joke **highlights a linguistic/cultural divide** and preparators’ **power over evidence**, and builds community (quotes on Currie’s slide ~pp. 151–152).  
**What it is for:** paradigm of **boundary work**—correct, but not Currie’s target phenomenon.

**KEY DISTINCTION A.**  
| Humor as… | Typical home | Does it explain “if it fits, I sits → Kanizsa study”? |
|---|---|---|
| Pedagogy / communication | Teaching, outreach, Ig Nobel | No |
| Boundary / community work | STS (Wylie) | No |
| Knowledge generation / creativity | Currie’s focus | Yes (target) |

**UPSHOT II.** Agree with the older literature *and* insist it misses the opening phenomenon.

### 04 // TOOLKIT — incongruity, revelation, play

### III.A Incongruity theory (instrumental use)

**CLAIM 4.** Currie uses **incongruity theory** (humor ≈ subverted expectation) **locally**: it need not be a complete theory of all humor; it only needs to capture the creativity-relevant cases.

**ARGUMENT 4.**  
(P1) A joke sets up an expectation set, then delivers a payoff outside that set.  
(P2) Grasping the payoff requires the right background knowledge.  
(P3) For Currie’s purposes, success = explaining creative scientific humor episodes, not winning the “essence of humor” debate.  
(C) Incongruity is a **working model**, not a full metaphysics of comedy.

**EXAMPLE 4 (childhood joke).**  
Setup: “Why did the plane crash?” Punchline: “Because the pilot was a tomato.”  
**What it is for:** classroom demo of expectation-subversion + background knowledge (planes, pilots, tomatoes).

---

### III.B Revelation (drawing on Jennifer Marra / “Mara” in transcript)

**CLAIM 5.** Getting a joke can be **revelatory**: by comparing/contrasting belief sets, humor exposes anomalies or unnoticed patterns in one’s thinking—and can thereby **open** philosophical / scientific problems.

**ARGUMENT 5.**  
(P1) Understanding humor involves juxtaposing belief sets.  
(P2) That juxtaposition can reveal errors, anomalies, or hidden expectations (sometimes with “relief”).  
(P3) Philosophy (and science) cannot solve a problem until it *notices* there is one.  
(C) Humor can function as an **epistemic trigger** for inquiry.

**EXAMPLE 5 (plane crash → beliefs about crashes).**  
You “learn” not chiefly that tomatoes crash planes, but that your crash-explanations were constrained in unnoticed ways.  
**What it is for:** move from “jokes are clever” to “jokes can teach.”

---

### III.C Playful distance and the “bit”

**CLAIM 6.** Humorous **play** is not opposed to seriousness: it creates **productive distance** from practical/noetic concern, enabling **exploration** (Morreall-style play-as-attitude; Kramer on subversive humor as serious-in-content, playful-in-mode).

**ARGUMENT 6.**  
(P1) Play-as-attitude suspends ordinary concern enough to “enjoy” / toy with a scenario (distance).  
(P2) Distance breaks ingrained thought patterns and invites collaborative expansion of a joke into a **bit** (“yes, and…”).  
(P3) Creative science needs exactly that expansion beyond habitual premises.  
(C) Humor is an **invitation to exploratory play** that can enlarge conceptual space.

**EXAMPLE 6A (joke → bit).**  
Joke: tomato pilot. Bit: “Weird—they were top of their class at flight school… Were other vegetables at flight school?”  
**What it is for:** show how play *extends* incongruity into a space of further questions.

**EXAMPLE 6B (lab play practices).**  
Exeter-ish cases Currie cites: biologists drawing cell division in odd ways (Wakefield / Gemma); Gail Davies–style mouse-lab engagement games—playful activities that reframe scientist–subject relations.  
**What it is for:** gesture at **practical** humor-adjacent interventions (without pretending the talk is a methods manual).

**UPSHOT III.** Humor’s creativity package = **incongruity → revelation → play-distance / bit-expansion**.

---

### 05 // CASE A — process: cats → Kanizsa

**CLAIM 7.** In the Smith et al.–style citizen-science story, humor is not garnish: incongruous funny cat behavior **generates the research question** and invites experimental expansion.

**ARGUMENT 7.**  
(P1) Background beliefs: cats like boxes; boxes are 3D; cats’ vision is relevantly like ours.  
(P2) Observed incongruity: cats sit in *2D tape squares*—funny *and* belief-clashing.  
(P3) Playful expansion (“the bit”): if real 2D outlines work, do **Kanizsa illusory** squares work? (plus real-square vs failed-illusion controls; owners in sunglasses to avoid gaze cuing).  
(C) Humor here scaffolds **discovery/pursuit**: question + design grow from incongruity + exploratory play.

**EXAMPLE 7 (three conditions).**  
Right: ordinary 2D box outline. Left: Kanizsa square illusion. Center: control / “failed” illusion. Result: informative about feline perception.  
**What it is for:** Currie’s paradigm of humor in the **process** of science (ideation / experimental framing), not only jokes in papers.

**UPSHOT IV.** This is the phenomenon Wylie/Ig Nobel stories do **not** capture.

---

### 06 // CASE B — product: Schrödinger’s funny TE

### V.A Form vs content; understanding vs bare argument

**CLAIM 8.** Following lines Currie likes (e.g. Alice Murphy): a good thought experiment’s **form** helps audiences **grasp** its content; humor can be part of that form—not a decorative extra.

**ARGUMENT 8.**  
(P1) Some views (e.g. Norton-style) treat TE content as separable argument; rivals treat TE form as argumentatively / understanding-relevant.  
(P2) Schrödinger’s 1935 “ridiculous case” is evocative, clear, and makes the anti-Copenhagen lesson salient.  
(P3) Currie: the **funniness** (silly framing, “diabolical device,” cat-proofing the apparatus, domestic cat vs quantum talk) is part of that form.  
(C) So: Schrödinger’s cat is not merely a TE that happens to amuse; it is a **funny TE**, and analysis should include the humor.

**EXAMPLE 8 (1935 paragraph cues — via 1980 translation on the slide/talk).**  
Schrödinger flags a “quite ridiculous” case; “diabolical device”; must be secured against the cat’s interference; living/dead “mixed or smeared out.”  
**What it is for:** show *authored* comic framing, not later pop-culture varnish.

**KEY DISTINCTION B (Currie’s corrective).**  
Popular gloss: paradox of “alive and dead.”  
Schrödinger’s emphasis (as Currie stresses): the cat in **superposition**—“mixed/smeared”—as a macroscopic percolation challenge to Copenhagen’s micro/macro split.

---

### V.B What the humor *does* epistemically

**CLAIM 9.** The humor juxtaposes belief sets (macro pets vs quantum weirdness), creates distance, raises questions (“is Copenhagen in trouble?”), and invites playful variants—thus aiding **understanding** and further exploration.

**ARGUMENT 9.**  
(P1) Incongruous juxtaposition reveals a problem that formal arguments alone may leave less graspable.  
(P2) Playful distance lets audiences turn the scenario over (physicists invent variants).  
(C) Humor contributes to the TE’s grasp-generating job.

**EXAMPLE 9 (slide bubbles).**  
“A cat in a superposition”; “What particles in superpositions are like”; “Is the Copenhagen interpretation… dumb?”  
**What it is for:** compress the revelatory + critical function of the joke-form.

**UPSHOT V.** Humor can matter in scientific **products** (not only outreach packaging)—at least for some TEs—while Case 1 covers **process**.

---

### 07 // COUNTERS — objections

**CLAIM 10.** Two quick objections do not sink the project: (i) humor’s dark side; (ii) thin direct evidence in the showcase cases.

**ARGUMENT 10A (dark side).**  
(P1) Humor can quash dissent, punch down, police hierarchies.  
(P2) Currie is **not** offering a complete ethics/sociology of humor.  
(C) Isolating an epistemically generative role is compatible with acknowledging abuse.

**ARGUMENT 10B (evidence / “is” vs “should”).**  
(P1) Objection: we lack smoking-gun ethnography that humor *did* the causal work in these cases.  
(P2) Currie’s thesis is normative: science **should** be funny (in the relevant sense); it could hold even if historical uptake were scarce.  
(P3) Published papers are post hoc rationalizations that scrub “silly” origins; STS often tracks power, not creativity.  
(C) Absence of neat documentation is expected—and motivates more open incorporation of humor, plus future empirical work.

**UPSHOT VI.** Remember the slogan: **not “science is funny,” but “science should be funny.”**

---

### 08 // MIRTH — epistemic emotion

**CLAIM 11.** **Mirth**—the affective uptake of (genuine) humor—is a bona fide **epistemic emotion**, analogous in role to **wonder** (cf. Helen De Cruz, *Wonderstruck*): it can signal that one “got” an incongruity and **motivate further inquiry**; it can be cultivated as an attitude/habit, not only as one-off jokes.

**ARGUMENT 11.**  
(P1) Epistemic emotions track knowledge-related states (curiosity, surprise, tip-of-tongue, etc.).  
(P2) Wonder = enticing glimpse of unknown terrain beyond current understanding → motivates investigation; can be inculcated as habit.  
(P3) Genuine mirth likewise tracks “sufficiently surprising / clever” incongruity and opens further questions; dad-joke **groan** closes them (obvious wordplay, little residual pull).  
(C) Mirth deserves a place beside wonder in the aesthetics–epistemology toolkit; “funny science” ≈ **mirthful** scientific culture, not mandatory punchlines in every paper.

**EXAMPLE 11 (dad joke contrast).**  
“What did the sand say to the beach? Nothing—it just waved.” → groan, not inquiry-driving mirth.  
**What it is for:** separate comic *failure modes* from epistemically live humor.

**UPSHOT VII (master chain).**  
Want creative science → need tools against conservatism + hard imagination → humor (incongruity) is revelatory + play-inducing → can shape TE form / understanding → mirth signals & motivates → **if we want creative science, we should have funny science.**

---

### 09 // SCOPE — normative strength (Q&A)

**CLAIM 12 (strength).** Currie does **not** claim humor is an **essential / indispensable** ingredient of all science, nor that science should be funny **always**. He claims: insofar as we want creative science, funny science should be **accommodated / encouraged**; and humor can drive good ideas **non-accidentally** because getting jokes is often epistemically sophisticated (contra “ideas can come from anywhere, so discovery is philosophically idle”).

**ARGUMENT 12.**  
(P1) Essentialism about humor-in-science would overreach and likely fail.  
(P2) “Anything goes” discovery dismisses better/worse idea-generation methods.  
(P3) Humor’s epistemic structure makes it a non-accidental creativity channel in the right contexts.  
(C) Moderate normative claim: encourage mirthful practice as one serious creativity tool among others.

**KEY DISTINCTION C (process vs product).**  
- **Product:** Schrödinger TE (humor in the public/scientific artifact).  
- **Process:** Kanizsa-cat ideation; lab-meeting / pub play; early hypothesis & design tinkering (discovery/pursuit more than justification/communication).

**KEY DISTINCTION D (which humor where).**  
Domain-heavy inside jokes fit **internal practice**; pedagogy/public communication need more accessible humor—don’t conflate the channels.

**PRACTICAL NOTES (Q&A).**  
- Trade-off: funny vs accurate (guard against distorting opponents/evidence).  
- Institutional resistance: “dry objectivity” culture.  
- Moral limits: some topics/contexts make humor inappropriate (gallows humor exists but is not a blank check).  
- Interventions: improv-like training, drawing exercises, informal ideation spaces—**social engineering of environments**, not “hire funny people”; scientists resist forced fun, so intervene carefully.  
- Humor as **relational / emergent**: funny-for-S depends on knowing enough-but-not-too-much (Schrödinger funny to the moderately informed; annoying to the specialist; opaque to the novice).

**UPSHOT VIII.** Moderate normative claim + non-accidental epistemic point; keep process/product and generation vs communication/boundary-work distinctions handy.
