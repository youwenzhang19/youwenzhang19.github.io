Handout: van Fraassen on the Pragmatics of Explanation

Lecture based on The Scientific Image, Ch. 5 (excerpt), from §III Asymmetry of Explanation (book pp. 166–192)

Text for this lecture: begin at “the asymmetry of explanation” (p. 166).
(The brief opening passage immediately before that section is not part of today’s assigned reading.)

Aim of the lecture. To reconstruct van Fraassen’s constructive account: what explanation is, once we abandon purely logical / semantic theories of explanation.

House style on this handout.
- CLAIM = thesis to remember
- ARGUMENT = numbered premises → conclusion (syllogistic skeleton)
- EXAMPLE = what the text uses, and what it is for
- UPSHOT = what we are licensed to conclude next

0. Target of critique (background, one box)

CLAIM 0. The theories van Fraassen opposes treat explanation as a context-free logical (or purely semantic) relation—roughly: if laws + conditions “cover” a fact, one has explained it (Hempelian covering-law spirit; more generally, “having enough information”).

ARGUMENT 0.
(P1) If explanation were only such a relation, sameness of logical form would suffice for sameness of explanatory status.
(P2) But we have persistent puzzles (asymmetry; “looks like an explanation but isn’t”).
(C) Therefore those puzzles are not fixed by more syntax; we need context / pragmatics.

(Today we enter the text where he develops this via asymmetry.)

I. Asymmetry of explanation (pp. 166–170)

I.A Diagnosis

CLAIM 1. Two classic troubles—(i) cases that seem to explain and seem not to, and (ii) asymmetry—are to be handled by contrast classes and contextually fixed relevance, not by a better deduction schema.

ARGUMENT 1.
(P1) A successful theory of explanation must dissolve both troubles.
(P2) If relevance and contrast are fixed by context, then (i) and (ii) become features of questions asked, not failures of covering.
(C) So the research program should be pragmatic: explain explanation via why-questions in context.

EXAMPLE 1 (polio / “local paralysis” type case, alluded to).
Formally one can write a covering derivation that looks explanatory yet feels empty or wrong.
What it is for: show that “has the form of an explanation” ≠ “is an explanation”—the missing piece is what is being contrasted.

I.B Testable corollary

CLAIM 2. If asymmetry comes from contextually selected relevance, then changing context can sometimes reverse or dissolve the asymmetry.

ARGUMENT 2.
(P1) Asymmetry = directional relevance under a contextually chosen relevance relation \(R\).
(P2) Context can change \(R\) (and the contrast class).
(C) Therefore the same pair of facts can, across contexts, support opposite explanatory directions.
(van Fraassen treats this as a decisive test for his approach.)

I.C Aristotle’s “sieve” of factor-types (pp. 166–168)

CLAIM 3. Even when \(A\) and \(B\) are equivalent given background theory, we can have “\(B\) explains \(A\)” without “\(A\) explains \(B\)”, because why-questions select a type of explanatory factor.

ARGUMENT 3.
(P1) Why-questions ask for a factor of a certain type (roughly Aristotle’s types of “because,” detachable from Aristotelian metaphysics).
(P2) \(B\) may be of the demanded type relative to \(A\), while \(A\) is not of that type relative to \(B\).
(C) Asymmetry can arise from type of relevance, not from a mysterious “explanatory force.”

EXAMPLE 3 (Posterior Analytics I.13 / four factor-types).
Interest in how an event is produced makes “because \(B\)” answer “why \(A\)?”, while “because \(A\)” fails for “why \(B\)?” if \(A\) does not describe a mode of production.
What it is for: give a positive micro-theory of asymmetry before the long story.

I.D Same sentence, different factor demanded — the hallway light (pp. 166–168)

CLAIM 4. Typographically identical why-questions can, in different contexts, demand different types of explanatory factor.

ARGUMENT 4.
(P1) Relevance = the factor-type selected in context.
(P2) The same interrogative sentence can be uttered under different selections.
(C) Hence “the” answer to “Why is the light on?” is not fixed by the sentence alone.

EXAMPLE 4 (hallway light; modernized lantern).
Father: “Why is the hall light on?”
- Ordinary context: “Because the switch is on / current flows” is cheeky; he wants something like “because we are expecting friends” (purpose / intention).
- Rewiring context (fear of short circuit): the mechanism answer is exactly what is wanted; wishes about guests are beside the point.
What it is for: (i) make contextual factor-types vivid; (ii) show when an answer may be rejected as the wrong type.

UPSHOT I. Correct answers exhibit a factor in a causal (or otherwise structured) network that context highlights. Next: a narrative where direction itself flips.

I.E “Tower and shadow” (pp. 168–170)

CLAIM 5. By changing context, one can overturn the usual asymmetry “tower height explains shadow length.”

ARGUMENT 5.
(P1) (from Claim 2) Context can reverse explanatory direction.
(P2) Tower–shadow supplies two contexts with opposite prominent factors.
(C) “Height explains length” is not a context-free law of explanation.

EXAMPLE 5 (layered story).
1. Narrator asks why the tower’s shadow is so long (with annoyance that it ruins the balcony tea).
2. Host’s answer: tower is 175 ft (commemorating Marie Antoinette’s age if she had lived to 1930) + straight-line sunlight / trigonometry → shadow length explained by height (geometric–commemorative interest).
3. Maid’s answer: the tower marks where he killed the maid he loved; he swore that at sunset the shadow must cover the balcony of their first declaration → height explained by the need for that shadow (motive–murder interest).
4. Further why: “Why kill her?” → “Because she flirted with the English colonel.”

What it is for: one story that simultaneously shows (a) multiple answer-types and (b) reversible explanatory focus for the same physical facts.

UPSHOT of Part I. Asymmetry and “pseudo-explanation” are symptoms of ignoring contrast + contextually fixed \(R\).

II. The model of explanation: master claim (p. 170)

CLAIM 6. An explanation is not a proposition, an argument, or a list of propositions; it is an answer. A theory of explanation is a theory of why-questions.

ARGUMENT 6.
(P1) What we call “explanations” are successful answers to why-questions in context (Part I).
(P2) Propositions that figure in answers are not identical with the act/role of explaining.
(C) Therefore the primary object of theory is the why-question (and its answers), not covering arguments as such.

EXAMPLE 6 (son / man).
Every son is a man and every man is someone’s son, yet “son” ≠ “man.” Likewise, explanatory propositions ≠ explanation-as-answer.
What it is for: block collapsing explanation into the propositions used when answering.

Agenda that follows: (i) context & propositions; (ii) general erotetic logic; (iii) why-questions proper; (iv) evaluating answers.

III. Context and propositions (pp. 171–175)

CLAIM 7. Natural-language sentences are typically context-dependent; a logic that assigns one proposition per sentence-type is too thin for explanation.

ARGUMENT 7.
(P1) Explanation tracks what question is asked in a situation.
(P2) What a sentence expresses (and what question it poses) varies with context.
(C) A theory of explanation needs an explicit context layer.

EXAMPLE 7.
- “I am happy now” is true in context \(X\) iff the speaker of \(X\) is then happy.
- “Twenty years ago this country could still have avoided a population explosion…” needs contextual time, place, modality.
- In the writing context, “I am here now” may express “van Fraassen is in Vancouver in July 1978.”
What they are for: motivate formal pragmatics before why-questions.

CLAIM 7′ (side clarification). “I am here” can be a priori true as a sentence in any context, while the proposition expressed need not be necessary.
EXAMPLE: the sentence is always true in context; “VF is in Vancouver” is contingent.
What it is for: keep sentence / proposition / modality distinct (needed later for presupposition).

CLAIM 7″. Context may include accepted theories, world-pictures, paradigms—the background against which questions arise.

IV. Questions in general (erotetic logic) (pp. 175–179)

CLAIM 8. Not every response to a question is an answer; we need a typology (direct / partial / complete, etc.).

ARGUMENT 8.
(P1) If every true proposition counted as an answer to every question, “answer” would be vacuous.
(P2) Ordinary practice distinguishes apt replies from answers of the right type.
(C) Explanation-as-answer requires that typology.

EXAMPLE 8.
- Peccavi telegram (Napier / Sind): witty response, not automatically a typed answer to “How goes the campaign?”
- Travel to Victoria: “Can one get there by boat or by plane?” — “Either” as pure direct answer; “Yes” as code; mentioning only boat as partial; adding “boat won’t make you late” as complete; shouting “Gorilla!” as barely relevant.
What they are for: discipline what will later count as a direct answer to a why-question.

CLAIM 9. Do not hastily identify a question with the set of its direct answers (Hamblin-style), on pain of collapsing distinct questions in classical logic.

EXAMPLE 9. “Is the cat on the mat?” vs. “Is relativity true?” — if a question is the disjunction of its answers, both become tautologies and the questions are no longer distinct.
What it is for: leave room for richer structure (the triple below).

CLAIM 10. Direct answers underwrite notions of inclusion, emptiness, absurdity, and presupposition; which question a wh-word expresses is itself highly contextual.

EXAMPLE 10. “Black hat or white?” presupposes at least one was worn; “Neither” can correct. Absurd questions only get absurd answers. “Which do you want?” needs a contextual domain (e.g. apples in the basket).
What it is for: toolkit for why-question presuppositions and legitimate rejection.

V. Why-questions (pp. 179–185)

V.1 The triple (through ~p. 181)

CLAIM 11. A why-question (in context) is determined by three elements:

\[ Q = \langle P_k,\; X,\; R\rangle \]

- \(P_k\): topic (the proposition at issue)
- \(X\): contrast class
- \(R\): relevance relation (when does \(A\) count as relevant to \(\langle P_k, X\rangle\)?)

Relevance is never “relevant to the topic alone,” but relevant to the topic as contrasted with the rest of \(X\).

ARGUMENT 11.
(P1) Part I showed that contrast and factor-type (\(R\)) change what is asked.
(P2) Erotetic logic requires structure beyond a bare proposition.
(C) The minimal adequate form is the triple \(\langle P_k, X, R\rangle\).

EXAMPLE 11.
- “Why is this conductor bent?” — topic: it is bent; contrast may be “this one vs that one” or “bent vs remained straight”; \(R\) may pick triggering events, standing conditions (strong field), or functional role.
- Yellow flame vs other colors; blood circulation: heart as pump (mechanism \(R\)) vs oxygen transport (function \(R\)).
- Adam and the apple: “Why the apple rather than the pear?” vs “Why eat rather than refuse?” — contrast class and requested reason intertwine.
What they are for: make each coordinate of the triple independently intelligible.

V.2 Direct answers and “because” (~pp. 182–184)

CLAIM 12. A direct answer has the form: Because \(A\), \(P_k\) in contrast to the rest of \(X\). The word “because” is not an irreducible modal; it mainly marks that \(A\) bears \(R\) to \(\langle P_k, X\rangle\) in this context.

ARGUMENT 12.
(P1) Direct answerhood requires: topic true; other members of \(X\) false; \(A\) true; \(A\) relevant via \(R\).
(P2) “Because” tracks that relevance claim.
(C) Same event can warrant “because” under one \(R\) and not under another.

EXAMPLE 12.
- Asking why Peter rather than Paul is paralyzed is defective if both are paralyzed (contrast presupposition fails).
- Getting up at 7:00 — if \(R\) is causal process, “because the milkman pounded” can use “because”; if \(R\) is motive, one may refuse “because” (“no reason… woke and got up from habit”).
What they are for: pin the semantics of direct answers; separate relevance from “goodness.”

V.3 Presuppositions and “arising in a context” relative to \(K\) (~pp. 184–185)

CLAIM 13. A why-question presupposes, roughly: (a) topic true; (b) only the topic is true in \(X\); (c) some true proposition is \(R\)-relevant to \(\langle P_k,X\rangle\). Crucially, whether a question arises in a context depends on background \(K\) (accepted theory + information)—not merely on all presuppositions being true tout court.

ARGUMENT 13.
(P1) We sometimes rightly reject a why-question as spurious.
(P2) That rejection is relative to what interlocutors accept (\(K\)).
(C) “Question arises” ≠ “presuppositions true in the actual world alone.”

UPSHOT V. Asymmetry and legitimate rejection of explanation-requests are already handled by the why-question theory; evaluation of answers is a further, separable topic.

VI. Evaluating answers (pp. 185–192) — secondary for “what explanation is”

CLAIM 14. How good “Because \(A\)” is can be scored along (at least) three dimensions: (1) acceptability / probability of \(A\); (2) how well \(A\) favors the topic against contrast members; (3) comparison with rival answers (probability, screening-off, etc.). These are evaluation tools, not the definition of explanation.

CLAIM 15. Evaluation uses not all of \(K\), but a part \(K(Q)\) that does not already smuggle in the fact to be explained (shared diagnosis with Hempel / Salmon-style approaches).

EXAMPLE (evaluation). If one already knows \(A\) and \(B\) with certainty, naive personal-probability tests like \(P(B\mid A)\neq P(B)\) collapse—so scoring must be relative to a suitably thinned \(K(Q)\).
What it is for: demote DN / statistical relevance from “essence of explanation” to “standards of better answers.”

VII. Closing moral (for the lecture)

CLAIM 16 (synthesis).
Explanation is a pragmatic achievement: answering a contextually identified why-question \(\langle P_k,X,R\rangle\). Logical covering may supply candidate answers; it does not constitute explanation.

One-line roadmap you can put on the board

Asymmetry & bad “explanations” (light, tower)
→ explanation = answer (son/man)
→ context-dependent questions
→ why-question \(=\langle P_k,X,R\rangle\)
→ direct answers / presuppositions / \(K\)
→ (only then) compare answers.
